Musical Taxonomy

Some people avoid learning music theory for the wrong reasons. A common justification for not learning music theory has to do with not wanting the magic to escape. While there is some truth to that, it varies greatly between people. Learning music theory is more about having a language, a way to communicate ideas to other people. It is also a way to communicate with your future self

How did that melody go again? If only past me had thought of future me a little more…

On the other hand, even professionals conflate music theory with music taxonomy. This is not intentional, but it does not help people learn. It doesn’t help that alternative “music theories” are a dime a dozen, similar to theories of physics, but that doesn’t mean they are all wrong.

Here I will offer clear definitions to distinguish between the two:

Music Taxonomy: the naming assigned to musical objects. Music Theory: melody, chords, relationships between sequences of notes, resolution, consonance/dissonance.

This distinction is important so that we can identify the issues with classical western music theory. One of the reasons notes are named the way they are is because music taxonomy is piano-centric. Most musicians have to learn complex finger arrangements for their instruments and sometimes they vary between different instruments. But what about guitar?

Guitar

I had played around with the idea of alternative notations years ago. It bothered me that chords were not represented exactly as they should be played.

“C Maj7 #11” should mean : [C E G B F#], but it is common to omit the third or fifth or to play different inversions. Especially for guitar, playing that exact chord without inversions is often not possible. Due to the hand shapes that can be made with standard tuning, a Maj7 is often played 1-5-7-3.

The Programming Approach

I’ve been programming since I was an undergrad but it wasn’t a priority since I was studying Electrical Engineering. I knew enough to use python instead of matlab throughout university, but it took me a while to get away from python. When I started trying to make JUCE plugins with C++, it was a struggle. Now I know enough to put myself in the anti C++ camp, but programming language roasting is not the purpose of this post.

When making a guitar practice application, a better solution to musical taxonomy came to me. It was absurd that 5 of the 12 notes have two names, and each of those two names has two characters. From a programming perspective, it is much simpler if all 12 notes are a single character. When spacing things out, nothing fancy needs to be done to make room for possibly 5 symbols: F#/Gb.

Since I had thought about this before from a guitarist’s perspective, I was very keen to find a solution. Another common issue with any new theory/taxonomy is how it fits into existing theory/taxonomy. I knew it would be best to not redo everything, but the black keys had to change.

I don’t remember exactly when it happened, but I made an analogy to commonly used math tuples (u,v) and (x,y,z).

This naming convention makes things a lot better for guitarists and programmers. It also allows for piano players to only relearn the black keys.

Another benefit for everyone is that these are also phonetically better. In the same way that people will say “sev” or “sen” when counting to 7 because it is one syllable, all of the new names for the black keys are a single syllable.
